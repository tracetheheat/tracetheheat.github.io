*Why where you live shapes your chances of recovering from addiction, and why Central Europe is a case of its own.*

{% include image.html url="/images/recovery-happens-somewhere-cover.jpg" href="https://unsplash.com/photos/cIhRe6S05KQ" caption="Photo by Damian Wyroslak on Unsplash" align="center" %}

Recovery from addiction has a geography, even though we rarely think of it that way. We usually picture recovery as something that happens inside a person, driven by willpower and by the support of family and friends. Yet recovery also happens somewhere, in a particular town and a particular healthcare system, either within reach of a clinic or far from one. Where a person lives can affect their chances before they ever decide to seek help.

<!--more-->

This blog looks at that geography in Central Europe, with special attention to Poland. Poland and its post-socialist neighbours spent decades under state socialism and then went through an abrupt transition to market economies after 1989. Their healthcare systems had to be rebuilt while that transition was still underway.

Addiction shows this history clearly, because for a long time the socialist state preferred not to see it. In Poland, drug addiction was officially cast as "alien to socialism"<sup>1</sup>, a disease of capitalist societies, and the authorities long refused to acknowledge it. The first help came from outside the system. Monar, founded by the psychologist Marek Kotański in 1978, was only officially approved by the regime in 1981<sup>2</sup>, and Poland passed its first law on preventing drug addiction in 1985<sup>3</sup>.

Alcohol was a different story, since heavy drinking was far too visible to deny. The state did recognise alcoholism, most notably through the 1959 Act on Combating Alcoholism<sup>4</sup>, but it met the problem with sobering-up stations, compulsory treatment and penal measures<sup>5,6</sup> rather than with care. Only in the 1980s and 1990s did addiction begin to be treated as a health problem with a treatment system of its own<sup>7</sup>. The old geography proved harder to change. By 1990, twelve of Poland's 49 voivodeships still had not a single addiction treatment bed, and many of the beds that did exist sat in remote rural institutions, placed there to serve compulsory treatment rather than patients<sup>5</sup>. Today's treatment infrastructure grew out of that upheaval, and research from Western Europe or the United States explains it only in part.

The blog also joins a community of English-language writers who cover Central and Eastern Europe. Among them are [*The Central European Blog*](https://komando1.substack.com/) by Marian Kechlibar, [*Not Lost, But Waiting To Be Found*](https://www.foundagain.eu/) by Kris Best, [*Ister*](https://ister.substack.com/) by Tomislav Bali, and [*Based in Bohemia*](https://www.basedinbohemia.com/) by Rob Cameron. Geographies of Recovery adds health and recovery to the topics they write about.

And who is behind the blog? I'm a geographer, and I currently run a [research project](/projects/geographies-of-recovery/) on spatial access to addiction treatment across Poland. I have no personal experience of addiction. What is personal is my long-standing interest in it, partly because geography, a field built around asking where things are and why, had mostly left this question alone. The longer version is on the [about page](https://geographiesofrecovery.substack.com/about) of the Substack.

In the coming months I'll post findings as the research develops and write about how the region's treatment infrastructure came to look the way it does. Some posts will compare countries, and some will react to books, films and reports from the region that rarely reach readers outside it.
